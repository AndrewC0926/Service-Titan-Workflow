Two architectures cover most of what you will sell. Everything else is a variation. The distinction that matters commercially: a packaged system puts the whole refrigeration cycle in one box on the roof, and a chilled-water plant splits it across a building, which is why the second one has more equipment in it and more lines on your card.

condenser water — heat leaving chilled water — 44°F supply conditioned air — 55°F supply
How this is built
The one sentence version Heat moves outward in stages. Room air gives its heat to chilled water at the air handler's coil. Chilled water gives its heat to refrigerant inside the chiller. Refrigerant gives its heat to condenser water. Condenser water gives it to the sky at the cooling tower.

A packaged rooftop unit does all four of those steps inside one cabinet, using outdoor air instead of a tower. That is the whole difference, and it is why a rooftop is cheaper and a chilled-water plant scales better.
{% elif active_tab == 'equipment' %}

Drawn the way a drawing legend draws them. Blue indicates water, green indicates air, orange indicates the hot side. Tags shown are the abbreviations used on a mechanical schedule. Lines listed are unverified — check the current DMG/ToroAire card before repeating one to a customer.

RTU

Packaged rooftop unit

Everything in one cabinet on the roof: fan, cooling coil, compressor, condenser, usually gas heat. Sits on a curb, ducts drop through the roof. The workhorse of retail, warehouse, small office and schools.

Sized in tons · 3–150 typical · life 15–20 yr
DMG: AAON is the flagship line here (unverified)

AHU

Air handling unit

A fan and a coil in a cabinet, fed by chilled water from a central plant instead of its own compressor. Lives in a mechanical room. Sections bolt together, so it can be built to almost any configuration, which is why custom AHUs are a real product category.

Sized in CFM · 2,000–100,000+ · life 20–25 yr
DMG: Energy Labs, ClimateCraft, BASX, Scott Springfield (unverified)

CH

Chiller

Makes cold water, typically 44°F, and sends it out to every coil in the building. Water-cooled types pair with a cooling tower and are more efficient; air-cooled types reject heat to outdoor air and skip the tower entirely.

Sized in tons · 20–3,000 · life 20–23 yr
DMG: ClimaCool modular, Geoclima (unverified)

CT

Cooling tower

Sheds building heat by evaporating a small fraction of the water passing through it. Cheap and effective, but it drinks water — which is why drought restrictions push owners toward adiabatic and dry-cooled alternatives that use far less.

Sized in tons or GPM · life 20–25 yr
DMG: Marley, Recold — both carry hybrid units (unverified)

VAV

VAV terminal box

A damper in a box above the ceiling that meters how much air each zone gets. Most carry a reheat coil, because the air arrives at one cold temperature for the whole floor and some rooms need it warmer. One per zone, so a floor has many.

Sized in CFM · 100–4,000 · life 20 yr
DMG: Titus and Nailor, plus the diffusers downstream (unverified)

FCU

Fan coil unit

A small AHU serving one room or one suite, fed by the same chilled water loop. Common in hotels, dorms, hospitals and multifamily — anywhere you want individual control without running a duct system to every room.

Sized in CFM or tons · 200–2,000 CFM · life 20 yr
DMG: Nailor, Engineered Comfort (unverified)

VRF

Variable refrigerant flow

Refrigerant piped directly to many small indoor units, each modulating independently. No ductwork, no water loop. Heat-recovery versions can cool one zone while heating another. Strong in retrofits where there is no room for ducts.

Sized in tons · 6–40 per system · life 15–20 yr
DMG: LG (unverified)

ERV

Energy recovery ventilator

Codes require outside air for occupants, and conditioning that air is expensive. An ERV passes exhaust and intake past each other through a wheel or plate so the outgoing air pre-treats the incoming air. Title 24 has made these near-standard on new work.

Sized in CFM · effectiveness 50–80% · life 15–20 yr
DMG: Aldes, and ERV sections inside AAON and custom AHUs (unverified)

B

Boiler

Makes hot water, typically 140–180°F, for heating coils and reheat. Condensing models pull extra efficiency from flue gas at low return temperatures. Title 24's heat pump baseline is steadily pushing new work away from gas-fired heating.

Sized in MBH · life 24–30 yr
Watch: SB 1206 and Title 24 both bear on replacement decisions

{% elif active_tab == 'numbers' %}

Six relationships cover most of what gets said out loud in a design meeting. Learn the first two cold; the rest you can look up without anyone noticing.

Ton of refrigeration

1 ton = 12,000 BTU/hr = 3.517 kW

The heat needed to melt one ton of ice in a day. Every capacity conversation runs on this.

Airflow per ton

≈ 400 CFM per ton

Comfort cooling rule of thumb, roughly 350–450. If someone says 20,000 CFM, think about 50 tons.

Sensible heat in an airstream

Q = 1.08 × CFM × ΔT

BTU/hr from airflow and temperature difference, at sea level. The 1.08 already folds in air density and specific heat.

Heat in a water loop

Q = 500 × GPM × ΔT

The water-side twin of the formula above. At a 10°F delta, chilled water works out to about 2.4 GPM per ton.

Condenser water

≈ 3 GPM per ton

Higher than chilled water because the tower rejects the building's heat plus the compressor's work, roughly 15,000 BTU/hr per ton.

Efficiency terms

COP · EER · IEER · kW/ton

Chillers are usually quoted in kW/ton, where lower is better. Rooftops use EER and IEER, where higher is better. IEER is the part-load number and is what actually matters, since equipment rarely runs at full load.

How this is built
Ranges, not values A number like 400 CFM per ton is a starting assumption, not an answer. Real values move with climate, occupancy, ceiling height and code. Quote them as approximations, name them as rules of thumb, and let the engineer supply the real figure — being the rep who says "roughly, and your calc will tell us" is worth more than being the one who sounded certain and was wrong.
